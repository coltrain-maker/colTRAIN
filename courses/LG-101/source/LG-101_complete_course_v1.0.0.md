- Read fertilizer and soil amendment labels without making unsupported claims.
- Ask useful questions about watering and common garden problems.
- Recognize when plant, pesticide, or environmental concerns need specialist guidance.

## 4. Full lessons

### LG-101-L01: Start with the Plant and Its Location
**Time:** 5 minutes  
**Objective:** Gather the plant and site information needed to help a customer.

**Why this matters**
The same plant may succeed in one location and struggle in another.

**Know the basics**
Plants need suitable light, water, air, growing medium, and temperature. Their needs vary by species. A tag or trusted growing guide gives better direction than a guess.

**Ask about the site**
Find out whether the plant will grow indoors, in a container, or in the ground. Ask how much direct sun reaches the location and whether water drains away.

**Use the plant label**
Check mature size, light requirements, and planting guidance. Climate suitability and seasonal timing can differ by region.

**Hardware-store example**
A customer wants a flowering plant for a shady porch. Ask how many hours of direct sun the porch gets before recommending a plant labeled for those conditions.

**What to say or do**
Say: “Is the space sunny, partly shaded, or mostly shaded? Let’s compare the plant tags.”

**Common mistakes**
Calling every bright location “full sun”; promising a plant will thrive without site details.

**When to ask for help**
Ask a garden specialist when a plant has unclear identification, unusual requirements, or local climate concerns.

### LG-101-L02: Know Soil, Potting Mix, Compost, and Mulch
**Time:** 5 minutes  
**Objective:** Explain what common bagged garden products are for.

**Why this matters**
Customers may use the word “dirt” for products with very different jobs.

**Garden soil and amendments**
Products sold as garden soil or soil amendments are intended for particular uses. Compost is decomposed organic material often used to improve soil properties. Always check the bag for intended use.

**Potting mix**
Container mixes are formulated for container growing and may differ from in-ground soil. A bag labeled for raised beds is not automatically the same as a container mix.

**Mulch**
Mulch covers the soil surface. It may help reduce evaporation and weeds when applied appropriately. Keep mulch away from direct contact with plant stems and trunks according to local guidance.

**Hardware-store example**
A customer is filling patio pots and grabs bags of topsoil. Explain that container potting mix is generally the category to compare and verify the bag instructions.

**What to say or do**
Ask: “Is this going in a pot, raised bed, or existing ground?” Then check the product label.

**Common mistakes**
Selling mulch as potting mix; assuming all bagged soil products are interchangeable.

**When to ask for help**
Ask for help with contaminated soil, drainage failures, or unusual planting media.

### LG-101-L03: Understand Fertilizer Labels
**Time:** 5 minutes  
**Objective:** Read basic fertilizer information without guessing at application rates.

**Why this matters**
Too much or the wrong fertilizer can harm plants and waste money.

**What N-P-K means**
A fertilizer label commonly shows three numbers for nitrogen, phosphate, and potash. The numbers describe the product’s nutrient analysis, not how many bags to buy.

**Match the use**
Products may be labeled for lawns, vegetables, flowering plants, or other uses. Some products also contain pesticides or herbicides and have additional legal label requirements.

**Follow directions**
Read the application rate, timing, coverage, and precautions on the actual label. Do not invent a rate or recommend using more than directed.

**Hardware-store example**
A customer asks whether a lawn fertilizer can be used on tomatoes. Check the actual product label for permitted uses rather than relying on the fertilizer numbers alone.

**What to say or do**
Say: “Let’s check whether this product is labeled for the plants you are growing.”

**Common mistakes**
Treating a high nutrient number as automatically better; guessing application amounts.

**When to ask for help**
Escalate suspected fertilizer burn, soil testing questions, and pesticide-containing products to qualified resources and label guidance.

### LG-101-L04: Ask Better Watering and Lawn Questions
**Time:** 5 minutes  
**Objective:** Help customers describe their watering and lawn-care situation.

**Why this matters**
Yellow leaves and brown grass can have more than one cause.

**Gather observations**
Ask what is changing, when it started, how often watering occurs, and whether the soil stays wet or dries out. Weather, drainage, light, and pests may all matter.

**Compare watering tools**
Hoses, sprinklers, watering cans, timers, and drip products serve different situations. Ask about the area, water source, and desired coverage.

**Avoid a quick diagnosis**
Do not assume every yellow leaf needs fertilizer or every brown patch needs more water. Help customers gather facts before selecting a treatment.

**Hardware-store example**
A customer shows a photo of brown grass. Ask about recent weather, irrigation pattern, pets, and whether the patch is spreading before suggesting a product.

**What to say or do**
Say: “What have you noticed about moisture and when did this begin?”

**Common mistakes**
Prescribing fertilizer for every yellow plant; claiming a photograph proves the cause.

**When to ask for help**
Escalate persistent plant decline, irrigation system changes, or suspected disease to a garden specialist or local extension resource.

### LG-101-L05: Know When to Seek Garden Expertise
**Time:** 5 minutes  
**Objective:** Recognize the limits of retail plant advice and label requirements.

**Why this matters**
A mistaken treatment can damage plants, people, pets, or the environment.

**Identify before treating**
An insect on a plant is not automatically a pest. A symptom may reflect disease, water, nutrients, weather, or another cause.

**Read pesticide labels**
If a product is a pesticide, its label specifies permitted sites, pests, application directions, and precautions. Do not recommend off-label uses or mixing products without label support.

**Use reliable help**
Ask for clear photos, plant names, and site information. Refer uncertain identification to a garden specialist, local extension service, or manufacturer support.

**Hardware-store example**
A customer wants insect spray for an unidentified bug on a vegetable plant. Pause and check pest identity and whether any proposed product is labeled for that crop and pest.

**What to say or do**
Say: “We need to identify the issue and read the label before selecting a treatment.”

**Common mistakes**
Suggesting an unlabeled pesticide use; assuming all insects should be eliminated.

**When to ask for help**
Escalate pesticide questions, suspected exposure, invasive species, and unclear plant problems to qualified resources.

**Key takeaways**
- An insect on a plant is not automatically a pest.
- If a product is a pesticide, its label specifies permitted sites, pests, application directions, and precautions.
- Ask for clear photos, plant names, and site information.

**Optional unscored practice**
LG-101-L05-P01. A customer wants a pesticide for an unidentified problem. What is best?
- A. Choose the strongest bottle

- C. Mix two products
- D. Apply twice the listed rate
**Answer:** B. Accurate identification and label instructions come before treatment.
