## 4. Full lessons

### IM-101-L01: Inventory Is More Than What Is on the Shelf
**Time:** 5 minutes  
**Objective:** Understand what inventory records mean and why accuracy matters.

#### Why this matters
Inventory is the merchandise a store has for sale. A product may be in the aisle, in a back room, on a display, or waiting to be received. The computer record is useful, but it is not a promise that an item is ready for a customer to take home.

#### Lesson content
**Three useful numbers.** On-hand means the quantity recorded as physically owned or held by the store. Available means the quantity that can be sold now under the store’s system rules. On-order means units expected from a supplier. These numbers can differ because of holds, pending transactions, receiving delays, or errors.

**Why it matters.** An incorrect count can lead to a missed sale, an unnecessary reorder, or a disappointed customer. Verify the item, size, and location before promising it is available.

#### Hardware-store example
A customer asks for twelve 3-inch deck screws in a specific package. The system shows twelve packages on hand, but only eight are on the shelf. The associate checks approved alternate locations and asks for help before promising twelve.

#### What to say or do
- Confirm the exact SKU, size, and package quantity.
- Check the approved inventory view and the shelf location.
- Explain what you can verify, not what you assume.

#### Common mistakes
- Treating an on-hand count as a guarantee of availability.
- Confusing pieces with packages.
- Promising incoming stock before it is received.

#### When to ask for help
Ask the inventory lead when a count, reservation, or shipment status is unclear.

#### Key takeaways

- C. The system must be wrong.
- D. Tell the customer to return tomorrow.
**Answer:** B. The visible six can be verified. Check other approved locations before promising more.

### IM-101-L02: Follow a Product from Delivery to Sale
**Time:** 5 minutes  
**Objective:** Describe the main steps in the inventory flow.

#### Why this matters
A product usually enters through an order and delivery. It is checked, received in the approved system, labeled or located, stocked, sold, and replenished when needed. Each step depends on accurate information from the previous step.

#### Lesson content
**Receiving is a checkpoint.** Compare delivered products with the store’s receiving paperwork or system. Confirm product identity, units, and quantities. Record exceptions using the store’s process. Do not assume every box contains the expected item.

**Stocking supports finding.** Place items in the correct approved location with readable labels. Keep the product identifier connected to the right shelf tag. A product placed in the wrong bay can appear missing even when it is in the building.

**Sales update records.** A completed sale usually changes the system’s stock record. Returns, transfers, damage, and corrections may also change counts. Exact timing and permissions depend on the store’s software.

#### Hardware-store example
A carton marked “12” contains twelve individual hinges, but the store sells them in packs of two. The receiver checks the SKU and unit of measure before entering a quantity.

#### What to say or do
- Check the item identifier and unit of measure.
- Follow the store’s receiving workflow.
- Set aside and report mismatches instead of guessing.

#### Common mistakes
- Receiving by carton count when the system expects eaches.
- Putting merchandise out before resolving an item mismatch.
- Leaving an unreadable shelf label in place.

#### When to ask for help
Ask a trained receiver or manager before posting a questionable receipt or correction.

### IM-101-L03: Stock Shelves with Accuracy and Care
**Time:** 5 minutes  
**Objective:** Apply basic stocking practices that protect people and inventory accuracy.

#### Why this matters
Good stocking makes products easier to find and reduces avoidable mistakes. It also keeps aisles usable and prevents damage. Follow the store’s safety rules and do not use equipment you are not authorized to operate.

#### Lesson content
**Match the label.** Read the SKU, size, color, and package count. Similar-looking products can be different. Compare the product to the shelf tag before placing it.

**Keep stock organized.** Use the store’s rotation rules when they apply. Keep heavy products in approved locations. Do not block exits, fire equipment, or customer walkways.

**Watch for damage.** Do not place leaking, broken, or unsafe products on the sales floor. Follow the store’s process for isolating and reporting them.

#### Hardware-store example
Two boxes of similar faucet connectors have different lengths. An associate checks both labels rather than placing them together behind one shelf tag.

#### What to say or do
- Match product and shelf tag before stocking.
- Keep merchandise within approved shelf limits.
- Report damaged or mislabeled items.

#### Common mistakes
- Stocking by appearance alone.
- Hiding extra product in an unrecorded location.
- Blocking an aisle with cartons.

#### When to ask for help
Ask for help with high, heavy, leaking, damaged, or uncertain merchandise.

#### Supporting materials

#### Key takeaways

### IM-101-L04: Investigate Inventory Differences
**Time:** 5 minutes  
**Objective:** Use a repeatable approach when shelf counts and system records differ.

#### Why this matters
A discrepancy is a difference between what the system says and what staff can verify. A missing item does not always mean theft. It may be in another location, not yet received, sold under another identifier, or counted in the wrong unit.

#### Lesson content
**Start with the exact item.** Check the SKU and unit of measure. Look at approved shelf, display, back-stock, and hold locations. Review available transaction details only if your role permits.

**Record what you found.** Use the store’s approved count or discrepancy process. State the quantity you physically verified and where you checked. Do not quietly change records or accuse anyone.

**Escalate adjustments.** Inventory adjustments can affect ordering and financial records. Only authorized employees should make them.

#### Hardware-store example
A paint roller refill shows six on hand but none in its assigned bay. The associate checks the endcap and back-stock location, then reports the discrepancy with the SKU.

#### What to say or do
- Verify the exact SKU.
- Check approved alternate locations.
- Document findings and request an authorized adjustment if needed.

#### Common mistakes
- Assuming missing stock was stolen.
- Changing counts without permission.
- Counting a similar item as the missing SKU.

#### When to ask for help
Ask the inventory manager before any count correction, suspected loss investigation, or unexplained variance.

#### Key takeaways

### IM-101-L05: Help Customers with Stock Questions
**Time:** 5 minutes  
**Objective:** Communicate stock information without making unsupported promises.

#### Why this matters
Customers want a reliable answer about whether they can buy an item today. Give the clearest verified answer you have. If the quantity is uncertain, say so and explain the next step.

#### Lesson content
**Separate facts from estimates.** A shelf check is a verified observation. An expected delivery date may be an estimate. Do not promise a shipment or hold unless the store’s policy and system support it.

**Offer a useful next step.** Check approved locations, request a stock check, or connect the customer with a team member who can confirm ordering options. Ask before suggesting a substitute because size or compatibility may matter.

**Close the loop.** If you promise to check, tell the customer what you found or who will follow up. Do not leave a customer waiting without an update.

#### Hardware-store example
A customer needs eight matching cabinet pulls. Six are confirmed on the shelf. The associate explains that six are available now and asks the inventory lead to check the remaining quantity.

#### What to say or do
- State the quantity you verified.
- Explain what still needs checking.
- Offer an approved next step or handoff.

#### Common mistakes
- Promising an arrival date without confirmation.
- Substituting a different size without checking fit.
- Saying “we have it” based only on a system count.

#### When to ask for help
Ask a supervisor about special orders, reservations, transfers, or uncertain delivery dates.

#### Supporting materials

#### Key takeaways

A customer needs eight pulls, but you can verify six. Which response is best?

- B. Six are here; I can check whether we have two more.
- C. You should buy a different style.

**Answer:** B. A clear verified quantity plus a next step builds trust.
