## 4. Full lessons

### PD-103-L01: Start with a Clean, Sound Surface
**Time:** 5 minutes  
**Objective:** Explain why preparation comes before painting.

**Why this matters**
Even a good coating can fail when the surface is dirty, loose, or wet.

**Inspect before coating**
Look for dust, grease, loose paint, damaged areas, and moisture. The customer should identify and correct underlying problems before painting.

**Clean as directed**
Cleaning methods vary by surface and coating. Use manufacturer instructions and allow required drying time.

**Do not paint over a problem**
Paint is not a fix for leaks, structural damage, or active mold. Old coatings may need special precautions.

**Hardware-store example**
A customer wants to paint over peeling paint on a damp wall. You recommend investigating the moisture cause before buying a new topcoat.

**What to say or do**
Ask: “Is the wall dry now, and do you know why the paint is peeling?”

**Common mistakes**
Assuming primer solves moisture; coating over loose material.

**When to ask for help**
Escalate suspected lead paint, mold, persistent dampness, and structural damage. Do not advise disturbing unknown older coatings.

**Key takeaways**
- Look for dust, grease, loose paint, damaged areas, and moisture.

**Answer:** C. The underlying condition must be addressed before repainting.

### PD-103-L02: Understand Patching and Sanding
**Time:** 5 minutes  
**Objective:** Recognize basic repair supplies and when they are appropriate.

**Why this matters**
Small defects may need repair before the final finish is applied.

**Match repair material to damage**
Spackling products, fillers, and patch materials have different intended uses. Confirm the surface, depth, and manufacturer directions.

**Sanding has a purpose**
Sanding can smooth a repair or prepare a surface when the coating instructions call for it. Grit numbers indicate abrasive coarseness; lower numbers are generally coarser.

**Control dust and exposure**
Follow product safety instructions. Do not recommend sanding suspected lead-containing paint or unknown hazardous coatings.

**Hardware-store example**
A customer has a small nail hole in painted drywall. You help them locate a patching product labeled for small interior wall repairs and the recommended finishing supplies.

**What to say or do**
Say: “How large is the hole, and is the surface drywall, wood, or something else?”

**Common mistakes**
Using wood filler for every wall repair; recommending sanding without checking hazards.

**When to ask for help**
Ask for help with large cracks, water damage, structural issues, or suspected lead coatings.

### PD-103-L03: Mask and Protect the Work Area
**Time:** 5 minutes  
**Objective:** Identify tools that protect nearby surfaces and control paint placement.

**Why this matters**
Preparation saves cleanup time and protects the customer’s belongings.

**Cover what should not be painted**
Drop cloths and protective coverings help protect floors, furniture, and fixtures. Select materials suitable for the location.

**Use tape correctly**
Painter’s tapes differ in surface compatibility and removal timing. Check the package instructions; some delicate surfaces need special care.

**Plan safe access**
Use appropriate access equipment and follow its instructions. Do not suggest unsafe ladder setups or work near electrical hazards.

**Hardware-store example**
A customer plans to paint trim beside a finished wood floor. You discuss floor protection and tape labeled for the adjoining surfaces.

**What to say or do**
Ask: “What surfaces need protection, and are any freshly painted or delicate?”

**Common mistakes**
Using any tape on any surface; forgetting floor protection.

**When to ask for help**
Escalate high work areas, damaged ladders, or work around exposed wiring.

### PD-103-L04: Select Brushes and Rollers
**Time:** 5 minutes  
**Objective:** Match application tools to the surface and coating.

**Why this matters**
The right tool makes application easier and can improve consistency.

**Brushes for control**
Brushes are useful for edges, trim, and smaller areas. Bristle material and shape should match the coating and task per product guidance.

**Rollers for broad areas**
Roller cover nap, material, and width affect application. Textured surfaces may need a different cover than smooth walls.

**Accessories matter**
Trays, grids, extension poles, and compatible applicators support the job. Always check the coating label for recommended tools.

**Hardware-store example**
A customer is painting a smooth interior wall and rough masonry. You explain why the same roller cover may not be ideal for both.

**What to say or do**
Say: “Is the surface smooth or textured? Let’s check the paint’s recommended applicator.”

**Common mistakes**
Assuming one roller works everywhere; choosing only by price.

**When to ask for help**
Ask a specialist about specialty coatings, spraying, or incompatible applicator materials.

### PD-103-L05: Plan Application and Cleanup
**Time:** 5 minutes  
**Objective:** Explain why timing, ventilation, and cleanup instructions matter.

**Why this matters**
The project continues after the first coat is applied.

**Follow application conditions**
Product directions may set temperature, humidity, ventilation, and recoating requirements. Check them rather than using a universal rule.

**Separate recoat from full use**
A surface may be ready for another coat before it is ready for cleaning or heavy use.

**Clean up by product instructions**
Water-based and solvent-based products can require different cleanup and disposal practices. Follow the label and local disposal rules; never advise pouring leftover coatings or solvents into drains.

**Hardware-store example**
A customer asks whether they can wash a newly painted wall tomorrow. You check the specific paint’s return-to-service and cleaning guidance.

**What to say or do**
Say: “Let’s read this product’s instructions for recoating, cleaning, and disposal.”

**Common mistakes**
Guessing dry times; suggesting disposal down a sink; ignoring ventilation.

**When to ask for help**
Escalate solvent hazards, spray applications, disposal uncertainty, or symptoms from exposure.
