## Employee-facing content

### 1. Today’s skill

Explain what a paint coverage statement means and identify conditions that can change actual coverage.

### 2. Why it matters

A customer may read a coverage number as a promise that one container will finish the project. Understanding the statement helps you explain what the number includes before discussing how much paint to buy.

### 3. Learn it

**A coverage statement describes how much surface a stated quantity of paint can cover under specified conditions.** It may also be called the **spread rate**.

For example, a label might read **“Coverage: 300–400 square feet per gallon, per coat.”** This means one gallon is estimated to cover that much surface with one coat under the stated conditions. Look for the area, paint quantity, and any wording such as **“per coat.”**

*This is an illustrative example; check the actual product’s coverage statement.*

Read these details together:

| Detail | What to check |
|---|---|
| **Surface area** | Is coverage stated in square feet or square meters? |
| **Paint quantity** | Is the rate per gallon, per liter, or for the entire container? |
| **Application basis** | Does it specify one coat, a recommended film thickness, or other conditions? |

**One coat means one application over the surface.** A statement of coverage per coat does not mean that one coat will produce the finished result the customer needs. Manufacturer guidance distinguishes surface coverage from the additional paint that may be needed for more coats. [Source 1]

Actual coverage can change with:

- **Texture:** Rough or textured surfaces can require more paint.
- **Porosity:** Absorbent surfaces can take up more paint.
- **Application method:** Brushing, rolling, and spraying can produce different product requirements.

Manufacturer coverage guidance identifies surface conditions and application method as factors affecting paint quantity. [Sources 2 and 3]

**Recommended action:** Read the exact product’s label or technical data sheet. Explain the stated quantity, area, and application basis before treating the figure as a starting point for an estimate.

**Common mistake:** Confusing **coverage** with **hiding**. Coverage concerns the area painted at the specified application. Hiding concerns how well the dried paint conceals the surface or color underneath. A surface can be painted without the previous color being fully hidden. [Source 4]

**When to verify or seek help:** Check product instructions when coat requirements, surface preparation, or the coverage basis are unclear. Ask a knowledgeable coworker or manufacturer representative rather than promising the highest stated coverage.

### 4. Customer scenario

A customer’s project has 300 square feet of wall surface. Their selected paint’s label states **“Coverage: 300–400 square feet per gallon, per coat.”**

They ask, “Does that mean one gallon is guaranteed to finish my walls, even if they need two coats?”

### 5. Quick check

**Question ID: CD-0011-Q01**

Which response best explains the statement?

A. Yes—the coverage range includes every coat needed to finish that area.
B. No—the rate applies to each coat; additional coats require additional paint, and actual coverage depends on conditions.
C. Yes—300 square feet is the lower number, so surface conditions cannot affect the quantity.
D. No—the coverage statement measures how well the paint hides the old color, rather than the area it paints.

### 6. Take it to the floor

Read **area + paint quantity + application basis** before explaining a coverage number.

## Employee-facing content

### 1. Today’s skill

Distinguish water-supply components from drain components by their basic function.

### 2. Why it matters

“Pipe under the sink” can describe several different parts. Identifying whether a component brings water to the fixture or carries used water away helps you start in the right product category.

### 3. Learn it

**Start with what the component does: brings water in or carries used water away.**

At a typical household sink, you will find both systems beneath the same basin.

| System | Basic function | Common under-sink examples |
|---|---|---|
| **Water supply** | Delivers water to the faucet | Shut-off valves and faucet supply connectors |
| **Drainage** | Carries used water from the basin toward the building’s drain system | Drain tailpiece, P-trap, and connecting drain tubing |

A **faucet supply connector** links the water supply to the faucet, often through a shut-off valve. Oatey describes its faucet supply lines by that connection and function. [Source 1]

A **P-trap** belongs to the drainage side. It is part of the path beneath the sink, not a connector supplying water to the faucet. Manufacturers identify sink traps as drain components. [Sources 2 and 3]

**Recommended action:** Ask, “Does that part connect to the faucet or to the drain opening beneath the basin?” A clear photo showing both ends and the surrounding connections can help establish its role.

**Common mistake:** Identifying a part only by color, flexibility, or location. Both systems occupy the same cabinet, and materials vary. Use the component’s function and connections rather than appearance alone.

Calling something a supply or drain component identifies its category—not the exact replacement. Size, material, connection type, and intended use still need checking.

**When to verify or seek help:** Compare the specific product’s documentation with the existing component before recommending a replacement. If a photo does not establish where a part connects, ask for more information or involve a knowledgeable coworker. Refer unresolved plumbing problems to a qualified plumber rather than guessing. This lesson does not instruct customers to disconnect or test plumbing.

### 4. Customer scenario

A customer places a curved plastic plumbing assembly on the counter and says, “I need another one of these. It came from under my bathroom sink.”

When you ask where it connected, they explain, “It was attached to the pipe coming down from the drain opening in the bottom of the sink.”

### 5. Quick check

**Question ID: CD-0012-Q01**

B. Sink drain components that carry used water away from the basin
C. Shut-off valves that control water going to the faucet

### 6. Take it to the floor

Ask **“water to the faucet or used water from the basin?”** Then verify the exact replacement.

## Employee-facing content

### 1. Today’s skill

Identify which light-bulb label measurement describes light output and which describes electrical power use.

### 2. Why it matters

Customers often ask for a bulb by its familiar wattage, such as “a 60-watt bulb.” With different lighting technologies, wattage alone does not reliably tell you how much light a bulb produces.

### 3. Learn it

**Use lumens to compare light output. Use actual watts to compare electrical power use.**

For example, an illustrative package might show **“Brightness: 800 lumens”** and **“Energy Used: 9 watts.”** These numbers describe different things.

| Label information | What it tells you |
|---|---|
| **Lumens** | How much visible light the bulb produces—the brightness measure used on packaging |
| **Actual watts** | The electrical power the bulb uses while operating |
| **“60-watt replacement” or similar wording** | A comparison with a traditional incandescent bulb, rather than the replacement bulb’s actual wattage |

The FTC explains that an LED can produce approximately the same light output as a traditional incandescent bulb while using fewer watts. Check the actual package rather than assuming every replacement has identical specifications. [Source 1]

**Recommended action:** When a customer asks for a brighter bulb, find the **brightness in lumens** on the package or Lighting Facts label. Compare that figure with the bulb they are replacing. More lumens means greater total light output; it does not guarantee identical lighting in every fixture. [Source 2]

**Common mistake:** Comparing an LED’s actual wattage with an incandescent replacement claim as though both numbers describe brightness. A package saying “60-watt replacement” may list a much lower actual wattage.

**When to verify or seek help:** Brightness is only one part of choosing a bulb. Before recommending a specific replacement, check its base, shape, voltage, and suitability for the fixture and controls against manufacturer information. If compatibility is unclear, ask a knowledgeable coworker or manufacturer representative.

Do not treat the replacement-equivalent number as the bulb’s actual electrical rating.

### 4. Customer scenario

A customer brings two bulb packages to the counter:

- **Bulb A:** 800 lumens; 9 watts.
- **Bulb B:** 1,100 lumens; 11 watts.

They ask, “Which one produces more light?”

### 5. Quick check

**Question ID: CD-0013-Q01**

Which answer uses the correct label information?

A. Bulb A, because its lower wattage means it produces more light.
B. They produce the same amount of light because both are LEDs.
C. Bulb B, because its higher wattage is the brightness measurement.
D. Bulb B, because 1,100 lumens is more light output than 800 lumens.

### 6. Take it to the floor

For **“How much light?”**, look at lumens. For **“How much electrical power?”**, look at actual watts.

## Employee-facing content

### 1. Today’s skill

Explain what a filter’s airflow arrow indicates and identify the information needed to establish its correct direction.

### 2. Why it matters

Customers may find an arrow on a replacement filter without knowing which way it should face. Understanding the marking helps you explain its purpose without guessing about their equipment’s layout.

### 3. Learn it

**An airflow arrow shows the intended direction of air traveling through the filter.** It does not automatically mean “up,” “down,” or “toward the room.”

Look for an arrow marked **“Air Flow”** or similar wording on the filter’s frame. Manufacturer guidance directs users to align it with the system’s airflow. [Source 2]

The direction depends on the filter’s location:

| Filter location | Direction to verify |
|---|---|
| At a typical furnace or air-handler return-side filter slot | Air travels through the filter toward the equipment’s blower. |
| Behind a return-air grille in a wall or ceiling | Air travels from the room through the filter into the return duct. |

Filtrete describes its arrow as pointing toward the blower. Guidance for a return-air grille specifies pointing the arrow into the duct. These describe the airflow path at different filter locations. [Sources 1 and 3]

**Recommended action:** Ask, “Where is the filter located?” Then use the equipment instructions, filter instructions, and any clearly identified airflow marking at that location to establish the direction.

**Common mistake:** Telling every customer to point the arrow upward—or copying the old filter’s orientation without checking it. The previous filter is a clue, not proof that it was installed correctly.

Do not aim the arrow toward the furnace’s physical location elsewhere in the building. The arrow follows air **through the filter at its installation point**.

**When to verify or seek help:** If the arrow is missing or the airflow path is unclear, check the exact filter and equipment documentation. Ask a knowledgeable coworker or refer the customer to an HVAC technician when the direction cannot be established. Avoid guessing from filter color or appearance.

This lesson explains the marking; equipment-specific access and replacement procedures still require the manufacturer’s instructions.

## Employee-facing content

### 1. Today’s skill

Identify the correct starting point for measuring common flat countersunk and pan-head screws.

### 2. Why it matters

Customers often bring a loose screw and ask for another the same length. Starting the ruler at the wrong place can lead to a different labeled length, even when the measurement itself is accurate.

### 3. Learn it

**Identify the head style before measuring length.** Common screw types do not all use the same starting point.

| Head style | Where measurement starts | Where it ends |
|---|---|---|
| **Pan head**, designed to sit above the material | Beneath the head, where it rests against the material | End of the screw |
| **Flat countersunk head**, designed to sit flush with the material | Top of the head, including the head in the measurement | End of the screw |

For example, a pan-head screw measuring **1 inch from beneath its head to its end** has a 1-inch measured length. For a flat countersunk screw, that same length measurement includes the head.

**Why the difference?** The general convention measures from the point where the installed material’s surface is intended to meet the fastener. A pan head sits above that surface; a flat countersunk head sits within it. [Source 1]

**Recommended action:** Place the ruler’s zero mark at the correct starting point, then read to the screw’s end. Record the unit along with the measurement.

**Common mistake:** Measuring only the threaded section. A screw may have an unthreaded section that still counts toward its length. Likewise, including a pan head adds material that is normally excluded from its labeled length.

Use the head’s shape—not its drive recess—to choose the starting point. A Phillips drive does not tell you whether the screw has a pan head or a flat countersunk head.

**When to verify or seek help:** Check the supplier’s dimensional drawing for unfamiliar or specialty fasteners. Do not automatically extend these two examples to oval heads, shoulder screws, or other designs. Ask a knowledgeable coworker when the measuring convention is unclear.

Matching length alone does not establish a suitable replacement; other fastener characteristics still need checking.

### 4. Customer scenario

A customer brings in a pan-head screw. Its head is designed to rest above the material rather than sit countersunk.

They ask you to measure its length so they can start looking for a replacement.

## Employee-facing content

### 1. Today’s skill

Distinguish common accessory shanks and recognize that fitting a tool’s holder does not establish suitability for use.

### 2. Why it matters

Two bits may have similar working ends but different ends that fit into the tool. Recognizing that connection helps you narrow the choices before checking whether the accessory suits the job.

### 3. Learn it

**The shank is the end of the bit held by the tool or accessory holder.** It is different from the working end that drills the hole or engages the screw.

For example, a Phillips driver bit can have a **¼-inch hex shank**. “Phillips” describes the screw-engaging tip; “¼-inch hex” describes the tool connection.

Start with these common shank types:

| Shank type | Recognition clue | Connection to investigate |
|---|---|---|
| **Round** | A cylindrical gripping section | A compatible adjustable-jaw drill chuck |
| **Hex** | Six flat sides | A matching hex holder or compatible drill chuck |
| **SDS-plus** | A specially grooved shank | An SDS-plus tool holder |

Bosch documents separate SDS-plus and three-jaw chucks for different accessory shanks. These are distinct connection systems, rather than interchangeable names for the same holder. [Source 1]

## Employee-facing content

### 1. Today’s skill

Explain the basic difference between annuals and perennials and recognize why local conditions affect whether a plant returns.

### 2. Why it matters

“Will this come back next year?” is a common plant-shopping question. Understanding life-cycle terms helps you answer clearly without promising that every plant labeled perennial will survive in the customer’s garden.

### 3. Learn it

**Annual and perennial describe a plant’s life cycle—not how long its flowers last.**

| Term | Basic meaning |
|---|---|
| **Annual** | Completes its life cycle, from seed to producing seed and dying, in one growing season |
| **Perennial** | Lives for more than two years under suitable conditions |

An annual does not necessarily live for a full calendar year. Its growing season may be much shorter. A perennial can live across multiple growing seasons, but that does not mean it flowers continuously or lives forever. NC State Extension distinguishes these life cycles. [Source 1]

**Climate changes the customer’s experience.** Some plants are biologically perennial but are grown as annuals where they cannot survive the winter. These are often called **tender perennials**. Their label may describe a perennial life cycle even though the customer would normally replace them each year in that location. [Source 2]

**Recommended action:** When a customer asks whether a plant will return, identify the exact plant and ask where it will grow. Check its hardiness information and local Extension guidance before describing its expected outdoor survival.

**Common mistake:** Saying “perennial means it always comes back.” Winter temperatures, drainage, and conditions at the planting site can affect survival. A perennial designation is not a guarantee. [Source 3]

Also, new seedlings appearing where last year’s annual grew do not mean the original plant survived. Some annuals leave seeds that produce new plants the following season. [Source 4]

**When to verify or seek help:** Check the plant tag and a reliable plant reference when the identity or local hardiness is unclear. Ask a knowledgeable garden-center coworker or local Cooperative Extension office rather than guessing from the display location.

### 4. Customer scenario

A customer points to a plant labeled **“tender perennial”** and asks, “Does that mean it will definitely come back outside next spring?”

You have not yet identified its hardiness range or asked where the customer plans to grow it.

### 5. Quick check

**Question ID: CD-0017-Q01**

B. “Yes—every perennial survives winter wherever it is planted.”
C. “No—tender perennial means it completes its life cycle in one growing season.”
D. “Yes—if new seedlings appear, that proves the original plant survived.”

### 6. Take it to the floor

**Explain the life cycle, then check local survival conditions.**

## Employee-facing content

### 1. Today’s skill

Distinguish sealing a joint from bonding materials and identify what to verify before selecting a product.

### 2. Why it matters

Caulk and construction adhesive can come in similar cartridges and use the same type of dispensing gun. Asking what the customer wants the product to do helps you identify the right starting category.

### 3. Learn it

**Start with the job: seal a gap or bond materials together?**

| Customer’s goal | Category to investigate | Basic purpose |
|---|---|---|
| Seal a joint or gap | Caulk or sealant suited to that location | Close the joint or gap with an appropriate sealing material |
| Attach one material to another | Construction adhesive suited to both materials | Create a bond between the surfaces |

For example, sealing a small joint between secured baseboard and a wall is different from attaching a loose piece of trim. The first is a sealing task; the second is a bonding task that may also require fasteners.

Caulk and sealant selection depends on conditions such as movement, moisture, indoor or outdoor exposure, and whether the product needs to be painted. [Source 1]

Construction adhesives also differ. A product intended to bond certain materials is not automatically suitable for every other material. **Substrates** means the surfaces or materials being joined. Manufacturer guidance directs users to choose an adhesive for the specific materials and construction requirements. [Source 2]

**Recommended action:** Ask, “Are you trying to seal this gap, hold these pieces together, or both?” Then identify the materials and location before checking a specific product’s label.

**Common mistake:** Choosing by cartridge shape or assuming that anything called “caulk” will attach a loose part. Packaging does not establish purpose or performance.

Some products are labeled for both bonding and sealing. Verify those claims rather than assuming the categories never overlap.

**When to verify or seek help:** Check the product’s intended uses, compatible materials, limitations, preparation requirements, and any fastening or support instructions. Do not promise that adhesive replaces required nails, screws, or other attachment methods. Ask a knowledgeable coworker or manufacturer representative when the label does not establish suitability.

### 4. Customer scenario

A customer says, “I need a tube of caulk for this trim.”

When you ask what they want to accomplish, they explain that a piece of trim has come loose and they want to attach it to the wall. Their immediate goal is to hold the piece in place, rather than finish a joint along its edge.

### 5. Quick check

B. Select whichever cartridge fits the customer’s dispensing gun.
C. Recommend filling the visible edge gap without addressing the loose trim.
D. Investigate a construction adhesive compatible with the materials, and verify any required fastening or support.

### 6. Take it to the floor

**Clarify the job first: sealing, bonding, or both. Then check the product.**

### 2. Why it matters

### 3. Learn it

Use these three parts:

| Part | Information to capture |
|---|---|
| **Product** | Description, size or package quantity, and an available identifier such as SKU, UPC, or item number |
| **Location** | Aisle and shelf, nearby landmark, or another description that makes the label easy to find |
