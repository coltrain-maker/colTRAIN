## Employee-facing content

### 1. Today’s skill

Distinguish common paint finishes by their relative shine and identify when to check the product’s sheen guide.

### 2. Why it matters

Customers may choose a paint color without realizing they also need to choose a finish. Understanding sheen helps you explain how shiny the dried paint will look.

### 3. Learn it

**Sheen describes the shine of dried paint.** Color and sheen are separate choices: naming a color does not identify its finish.

Use this general progression to explain common finish names:

| Finish | General appearance |
|---|---|
| Flat or matte | Little to very little visible shine |
| Eggshell | A soft, low shine |
| Satin | More noticeable shine than eggshell |
| Semi-gloss | A distinctly shiny finish |
| Gloss or high-gloss | The shiniest end of the range |

This is a guide to relative appearance, not a universal measurement chart. Flat and matte can be separate finishes. Manufacturers may also use names such as pearl, low lustre, or soft gloss. Check the selected product’s guide before treating those names as equivalent. [Source 1]

**Recommended action:** Ask, “How much shine would you like?” Then use the manufacturer’s finish guide or an available physical sheen sample to clarify the choices.

**Common mistake:** Promising cleaning performance from the sheen name alone. The paint formulation also matters; some low-sheen products are designed for washability. Confirm the specific product’s claims before making that promise. [Source 2]

**When to verify or seek help:** Check the product label, manufacturer’s guide, or technical data sheet when comparing unfamiliar finish names or answering performance questions. If the information does not resolve the question, ask a knowledgeable coworker or manufacturer representative.

### 4. Customer scenario

A customer has selected a wall color. Within the same paint line, the store offers flat, eggshell, satin, and semi-gloss.

The customer says, “I want a little shine—more than flat, but less than satin.”

### 5. Quick check

**Question ID: CD-0001-Q01**

Which finish best matches the customer’s description?

A. Semi-gloss
B. Flat
C. Eggshell
D. Satin

### 6. Take it to the floor

Use finish names to explain relative shine. Use the specific product’s information to confirm performance.

## Employee-facing content

### 1. Today’s skill

Distinguish four common plumbing connection types using their joining features, without assuming that similar-looking parts are compatible.

### 2. Why it matters

A customer may call every connection that turns a “screw-on fitting.” Recognizing how the connection actually joins helps you ask better questions before selecting a replacement.

### 3. Learn it

Start with **how the parts join**, rather than the fitting’s color or overall shape.

| Connection type | Recognition clue |
|---|---|
| **Threaded pipe connection** | External threads on one part screw into matching internal threads on another. |
| **Slip joint** | Common on tubular sink drains: a nut tightens around a tube, with a washer providing the seal. |
| **Solvent weld** | A plastic pipe fits into a socket designed for joining with the appropriate solvent cement. |
| **Push-to-connect** | A compatible pipe pushes into a fitting with an internal gripping and sealing mechanism. |

Threaded pipe connections and solvent-welded connections use different joining methods. Solvent welding chemically joins compatible plastic surfaces; it is not simply a friction fit. [Source 1]

**Recommended action:** Identify the connection at **each end**. One adapter can have a slip-joint connection at one end and a solvent-weld socket at the other. Naming one end does not describe the entire fitting. [Source 2]

**Common mistake:** Seeing threads on a slip-joint nut and treating it as a threaded pipe connection. Look at the whole assembly: the tube, nut, and sealing washer.

Push-to-connect fittings may hide their working parts. For example, SharkBite describes an internal grab ring that holds the pipe and an O-ring that seals it. Do not assume every push fitting accepts the same pipe materials. [Source 3]

**When to verify or seek help:** Before recommending a replacement, confirm pipe material, size, connection type, and intended use against the specific product’s instructions. A smooth socket alone does not prove that a fitting is intended for solvent welding. If identification remains uncertain, ask a knowledgeable coworker or manufacturer representative.

### 4. Customer scenario

A customer brings in a loose nut and washer from beneath a bathroom sink. Their photo shows a smooth drain tube passing through the nut into another fitting.

They ask, “What kind of connection should I look for?”

### 5. Quick check

**Question ID: CD-0002-Q01**

Which connection type best matches the assembly shown and described?

A. Slip joint
B. Threaded pipe connection
C. Solvent weld
D. Push-to-connect

### 6. Take it to the floor

Name the connection at each end before matching the replacement. Connection type is one part of the match—not proof of compatibility.

## Employee-facing content

### 1. Today’s skill

Identify which label markings describe voltage, electric current, and electrical power.

### 2. Why it matters

Electrical labels can place several numbers close together. Knowing what each unit means helps you explain the label without confusing a product’s voltage with its current or power.

### 3. Learn it

**Read the unit beside the number.** The number alone does not tell you what is being measured.

| Label marking | What it measures | Plain-language meaning |
|---|---|---|
| **V — volts** | Voltage | The electrical potential difference, sometimes described as electrical “push.” |
| **A — amperes, or amps** | Electric current | The rate at which electric charge flows. |
| **W — watts** | Power | The rate at which energy is transferred or used. |

These units describe different quantities. They are related, but their numbers are not interchangeable. [Sources 1 and 2]

**Recommended action:** Read both the number and its unit aloud: “This marking says eight amps.” Then check the surrounding label text to understand what that rating describes.

For a device that uses an adapter, pay attention to **input** and **output**. Input describes what the device or adapter receives; output describes what it supplies. Do not combine numbers from different parts of a label.

**Common mistake:** Calling every electrical number “wattage.” A number followed by **A** is an amperage marking, even when a wattage marking appears nearby.

Watts also differ from **kilowatt-hours (kWh)**. Watts describe power; kilowatt-hours describe energy used over time. A wattage number alone does not tell you the total energy a customer will use. [Source 3]

**When to verify or seek help:** If a marking is missing, unclear, or unfamiliar, check the exact model’s manufacturer instructions. Identifying these units does not establish that a cord, adapter, outlet, or circuit is suitable. Refer compatibility questions to the product documentation and unresolved wiring or circuit questions to a qualified electrician.

### 4. Customer scenario

A customer shows you a product label with several electrical markings. One line reads **“Rated current: 8 A.”**

The customer asks, “What does that line tell me?”

### 5. Quick check

**Question ID: CD-0003-Q01**

What does the **8 A** marking describe?

A. The product’s rated voltage
B. The product’s rated power
C. The product’s energy use over eight hours
D. The product’s rated electric current

### 6. Take it to the floor

Read the number, unit, and surrounding label text together before explaining an electrical rating.

## Employee-facing content

### 1. Today’s skill

Distinguish nominal filter size from actual dimensions and identify the information needed to verify a replacement.

### 2. Why it matters

A customer may notice that a filter’s measurements differ from the size printed prominently on its package. Explaining the two size descriptions helps you investigate the fit without assuming the filter is mislabeled.

### 3. Learn it

**Air filters can have a nominal size and actual dimensions.**

| Size description | What it means |
|---|---|
| **Nominal size** | The designated size used to identify and shop for the filter. It is often rounded. |
| **Actual dimensions** | The filter’s physical measurements, which may differ from its nominal size. |

**Why are they different?** Nominal sizes provide convenient shopping labels. Many filters are manufactured slightly smaller than their nominal dimensions to allow clearance when inserting and removing them from the intended slot. [Source 4]

However, **there is no universal amount to subtract**. Some filters—especially filters designed for specific cabinets—have actual dimensions that differ in other ways. Use the manufacturer’s published measurements rather than assuming every “one-inch” or “four-inch” filter measures exactly that thickness. [Sources 2 and 3]

Filter dimensions describe the two sides of the face and the depth, or thickness. Read all three numbers and their units. A matching face size alone does not establish a matching depth. Manufacturer product information distinguishes nominal sizes from actual measurements. [Source 1]

**Recommended action:** Ask for the complete size marking and any filter model or part number. A clear photo of the existing filter label can help. Then compare the proposed replacement’s published dimensions and compatibility information with the equipment or filter cabinet’s requirements.

Treat an old filter as a useful clue, rather than proof that it was the correct replacement.

**Common mistake:** Assuming that every filter with the same nominal size has identical physical dimensions. Filtrete specifically advises checking actual frame size because nominal descriptions can differ by brand or system. [Source 2]

**When to verify or seek help:** Check the exact replacement’s manufacturer information when changing brands, comparing depths, or resolving conflicting measurements. If the label and equipment requirements do not establish a match, ask a knowledgeable coworker or manufacturer representative. Unresolved equipment-fit questions may need an HVAC technician.

Do not promise a fit by rounding measurements alone. Size identification is the first step; it does not settle every question about filter suitability.

### 4. Customer scenario

A customer brings a photo of a filter package. It shows a nominal size and a separate set of actual dimensions. The numbers are slightly different.

The customer asks, “Which information should we use to check whether a replacement fits?”

### 5. Quick check

**Question ID: CD-0004-Q01**

Which response is most accurate?

A. Match the nominal face dimensions; the depth does not affect fit.
B. Use the nominal size to locate candidates, then verify actual dimensions and equipment compatibility.
C. Round the actual dimensions to whole inches and select the nearest filter.
D. Choose any brand with the same nominal size; physical dimensions will be identical.

### 6. Take it to the floor

Use nominal size to start the search. Verify the replacement using all three dimensions and the equipment requirements.

## Employee-facing content

### 1. Today’s skill

Match common screw-drive shapes to the corresponding hand screwdriver and recognize when tip size still needs confirmation.

### 2. Why it matters

A screwdriver can look close to the right choice without fitting correctly. Matching both drive type and tip size helps reduce slipping and damage to the screw or tool.

### 3. Learn it

**Match the drive shape first, then confirm the tip size.** The drive is the slot or recess where the screwdriver engages—not the screw head’s outer shape.

Start with these common patterns:

| Drive type | Recognition clue | Matching hand tool |
|---|---|---|
| **Slotted** | One straight slot across the screw head | Slotted screwdriver, commonly called a **flathead**, **flathead screwdriver**, or **flat-blade screwdriver** |
| **Phillips** | A cross-shaped recess | Phillips screwdriver |
| **Square** | A square recess | Square-drive screwdriver |
| **TORX** | A six-lobed, star-like recess | Correctly sized TORX screwdriver |

**Why “slotted”?** The name describes the straight slot that receives the screwdriver’s blade. Customers commonly call the matching tool a **flathead**—recognize both names. When discussing screws, keep drive type separate from head shape: “slotted” identifies where the tool engages, while “flat head” can describe the screw’s outer head shape. [Sources 6 and 7]

These are starting clues. Unfamiliar, combination, or specialty drives need closer identification before you choose a tool.

**Recommended action:** Check the screw package or manufacturer information for the specified drive type and size. Then match that designation to the screwdriver’s marking. Shape identifies the family; it does not establish the size.

For example, Phillips screwdrivers come in different tip sizes. Choosing “a Phillips” is only the first step. Klein Tools advises matching both screwdriver type and size to the screw. [Source 1]

**Common mistake:** Choosing by handle size or shaft length. A longer screwdriver does not necessarily have a larger tip. Read the tip designation.

**When to verify or seek help:** If a tip will not seat properly or slips, stop and recheck the type, size, and condition rather than forcing it. Ask a knowledgeable coworker when the drive is unfamiliar or damaged.

Some cross-shaped drives, including Phillips and Pozidriv, have separate tool designations. Likewise, do not assume every star-like recess uses the same driver. Verify the exact designation. [Sources 3 and 5]

### 4. Customer scenario

A customer is assembling a cabinet by hand. The screw package specifies **“Phillips #2.”** They have found the Phillips screwdrivers but see several tip sizes and shaft lengths.

They ask which detail to match before choosing one.

### 5. Quick check

**Question ID: CD-0005-Q01**

Which selection best matches the package specification?

A. A Phillips screwdriver with the longest shaft
B. A square-drive screwdriver marked #2
C. A Phillips screwdriver with a #2 tip
D. A Phillips screwdriver with the largest handle

### 6. Take it to the floor

Match **drive type + tip size**. Choose shaft length and handle features after establishing the correct tip.

## Employee-facing content

### 1. Today’s skill

Distinguish the basic uses of a drill/driver and an impact driver.

### 2. Why it matters

Both tools can drive screws, so customers may assume they do the same job. Explaining their basic differences helps customers understand why a combination kit includes both.

### 3. Learn it

**A drill/driver is a general-purpose drilling and screwdriving tool. An impact driver is primarily a fastening tool.**

| Tool | Basic use | Common identifying feature |
|---|---|---|
| **Drill/driver** | Drilling holes and driving screws | An adjustable chuck with jaws that tighten around a compatible bit |
| **Impact driver** | Driving fasteners, including demanding screwdriving tasks | A quick-change holder commonly accepting compatible ¼-inch hex-shank accessories |

A drill/driver turns the bit continuously. Many models have an **adjustable clutch** for screwdriving. The clutch limits the turning force delivered at the selected setting, helping control how deeply a screw is driven. It does not guarantee that a screw cannot be overdriven. Follow the model’s instructions for selecting drilling or screwdriving settings. [Source 1]

An impact driver adds **repeated twisting blows** when its impact mechanism operates. These rotational impacts help with demanding fastening work. That makes it useful for tasks such as driving longer screws, subject to the tool’s and fastener manufacturer’s instructions. [Source 2]

**Recommended action:** Start by asking, “Are you mainly making holes, driving fasteners, or doing both?” Use the answer to explain the tools before comparing individual models.

**Common mistake:** Saying a drill cannot drive screws or an impact driver cannot drill holes. Their capabilities overlap. Some impact drivers can drill with suitable accessories, but accessory fit, ratings, and permitted applications must be checked. [Source 3]

**When to verify or seek help:** Check the exact tool manual and accessory instructions before recommending a bit or application. Confirm that accessories are suitable for impact use when applicable. If compatibility is unclear, ask a knowledgeable coworker or manufacturer representative.

### 4. Customer scenario

A customer is looking at a kit containing a drill/driver and an impact driver. They plan to make holes in wood and install screws.

They ask, “Why would I want two tools that look so similar?”

### 5. Quick check

## Employee-facing content

### 1. Today’s skill

Explain what a fertilizer’s three analysis numbers represent without treating them as a complete product recommendation.

### 2. Why it matters

Customers may assume larger fertilizer numbers mean a better product. Reading the analysis correctly helps you explain what is in the package before discussing whether it fits the customer’s needs.

### 3. Learn it

**The three numbers describe nutrient percentages by weight, in a fixed order.** They are commonly called the **N-P-K analysis**.

| Position | Nutrient represented | U.S. label reporting basis |
|---|---|---|
| **First — N** | Nitrogen | Total nitrogen |
| **Second — P** | Phosphorus | Available phosphate, expressed as P₂O₅ |
| **Third — K** | Potassium | Soluble potash, expressed as K₂O |

You do not need to memorize the chemical formulas to explain the order. Recognize that “phosphate” and “potash” are the reporting terms customers may see in the guaranteed analysis. The second and third numbers are not percentages of pure elemental phosphorus and potassium. [Sources 1 and 2]

For example, **10-10-10** means 10% nitrogen, 10% phosphate, and 10% potash by product weight. It does **not** mean ten pounds of each in every bag. The bag’s total weight determines the pounds represented by those percentages.

**Recommended action:** Point to the three numbers, explain their order, and locate the **guaranteed analysis** for more detail. Treat the numbers as a description of nutrient content.

**Common mistake:** Saying the highest numbers identify the best fertilizer. The analysis alone does not tell you whether those nutrients are needed, how quickly they become available, or how much product to apply.

**When to verify or seek help:** Before recommending a product, ask what the customer is growing and whether they have a soil-test recommendation. Check the product label for intended uses and application directions. Soil-test guidance helps identify nutrient needs; unresolved questions can go to a knowledgeable coworker or local Cooperative Extension office. [Source 3]

### 4. Customer scenario

A customer points to **10-10-10** on a fertilizer bag and asks:

“Does that mean this bag contains ten pounds each of nitrogen, phosphate, and potash?”

### 5. Quick check

**Question ID: CD-0007-Q01**

Which response correctly explains the numbers?

A. Yes—the numbers show pounds of each nutrient, regardless of bag weight.
B. No—the numbers show the recommended pounds to apply per garden.
C. Yes—but only when all three numbers are equal.
D. No—they show percentages by weight of nitrogen, phosphate, and potash, in that order.

### 6. Take it to the floor

Explain **what the numbers mean** before discussing **whether the product meets the plant’s needs**.

## Employee-facing content

### 1. Today’s skill

Distinguish the basic sealing locations served by door-frame weatherstripping and door sweeps.

### 2. Why it matters

A customer asking for “something to stop a door draft” may need help identifying where air is getting through. Starting with the gap’s location helps you guide them toward the right product category.

### 3. Learn it

**Ask where the gap is before choosing a seal.** For a typical swinging exterior door, separate the top and sides from the bottom.

| Gap location | Product category to investigate | Basic purpose |
|---|---|---|
| Between the closed door and its frame at the top or sides | Door-frame weatherstripping | Forms a seal where the door meets the frame |
| Beneath the door | Door sweep or another compatible door-bottom seal | Helps seal the gap along the bottom edge |

Door sweeps are part of the broader weatherstripping family. In a customer conversation, “frame weatherstripping” and “door sweep” help distinguish their locations. Building guidance identifies perimeter weatherstripping at the top and sides and a sweep along the bottom. [Source 1]

**Recommended action:** Ask, “Is the draft coming around the top or sides, or underneath?” Then ask about the existing seal and request a clear photo or product information when needed.

**Common mistake:** Treating every draft as a reason to add more material. Weatherstripping should seal when the door closes while still allowing the door to operate properly. A product that prevents normal closing is not a successful match. [Source 2]

Location narrows the search; it does not identify the exact replacement. Door construction, the existing attachment method, gap size, and the threshold—the piece beneath the doorway—can affect the choice.

**When to verify or seek help:** Check the seal manufacturer’s fit and installation information before recommending a specific product. If the door rubs, sags, or will not latch, avoid promising that a new seal will fix it. Ask a knowledgeable coworker or refer the customer to a door professional when the cause remains unclear.

### 4. Customer scenario

A customer says the flexible sealing strip along the latch side of their exterior door is torn. They want to replace that strip.

They ask which aisle category to start with.

### 5. Quick check

A. Door sweeps for the bottom edge
B. Door-frame weatherstripping for the sides and top
C. Replacement thresholds beneath the doorway
D. Door-bottom shoes that fit around the lower edge

## Employee-facing content

### 1. Today’s skill

Recognize an aisle trip hazard and select an immediate response that protects people while following store procedure.

### 2. Why it matters

An ordinary stocking item can become a hazard when it enters someone’s walking path. Responding when you notice it helps protect customers and coworkers before someone trips.

### 3. Learn it

**Look at the walking path, not just the object.** An empty box may be harmless in its designated storage location but create a trip hazard in an aisle.

Common examples include loose packaging, cords across walkways, and clutter where people need to step. NIOSH recommends keeping walkways clear of these obstacles. [Source 1]

**Recommended action:** When you spot a hazard, address people’s immediate exposure:

- **If you can safely remove a simple obstruction within your training and store procedure,** move it to its designated location and check that the path is clear.
- **If you cannot safely correct it immediately,** keep people away using the store’s hazard-control procedure and call for help. Do not leave the area unprotected while seeking assistance.

OSHA’s workplace guidance requires hazardous walking surfaces to be corrected or, when correction cannot happen immediately, guarded against use. [Source 2]

**Common mistake:** Assuming that reporting the problem means the hazard has been controlled. A message to a coworker does not remove an obstruction or prevent someone from walking into it.

Choose a response that does not create another hazard. Moving a box into the next aisle simply relocates the problem. Avoid trying to move an unstable load or handling something unfamiliar just to clear the path quickly.

**When to verify or seek help:** Ask for assistance when correction involves heavy or unstable items, damaged flooring, equipment, or an unknown substance. Follow the store’s instructions for barriers, reporting, and reopening the area. This lesson does not authorize repairs or unfamiliar cleanup work.

## Employee-facing content

### 1. Today’s skill

Identify the information a coworker needs to continue an unfinished customer task.

### 2. Why it matters

A customer’s request may outlast your shift. A clear handoff helps the next employee continue the work without making the customer start over or guessing what was promised.

### 3. Learn it

**A useful handoff explains where the task stands and what needs to happen next.**

Use these five prompts:

| Prompt | Information to include |
|---|---|
| **What is the request?** | The customer’s goal and relevant product or order reference |
| **What is already done?** | Completed checks, actions, and confirmed findings |
| **What is still open?** | Missing information or an unresolved decision |
| **What happens next?** | The specific next action and any actual commitment already made |
| **Who is taking over?** | The person accepting responsibility, or the fact that acceptance is still needed |

**Recommended action:** Communicate the handoff in a way the receiving coworker can access and understand. Options include a direct conversation, a written note, a shared shift log, an internal message, or a note in an existing customer or order record. Follow store procedures where they exist.

For a verbal handoff, ask the receiving coworker to confirm the next action. If you leave a note or send a message, confirm they received it and accepted responsibility rather than assuming the task has transferred.

Keep the handoff short and factual. Separate confirmed information from anything that still needs checking. For example, “Supplier availability not yet confirmed” is clearer than “Should be here soon.”

Include a follow-up time only when one was actually agreed upon. Share customer details only with coworkers who need them, using a method appropriate for that information.

**Common mistake:** Writing “Please handle this” without identifying the next action or confirming who will take it. A person’s name in a note does not establish that they have accepted the task.

When possible, confirm that the receiving coworker understands and accepts the handoff. If no one has accepted it, use the store’s usual method, or ask a supervisor if no process is established, instead of treating the task as transferred.

**When to verify or seek help:** Check the order record or original message when a price, product detail, or customer commitment is uncertain. Ask a supervisor when responsibility remains unclear. Reference existing customer records where available, using the store’s usual method, or ask a supervisor if no process is established. Avoid copying unnecessary personal details into a general shift note.
