### HB-103-L01: Understand Basic Heating and Cooling Systems (5 minutes)

**Objective:** Recognize common HVAC categories

**Why this matters:** Customers may describe comfort problems without knowing their equipment type.

#### Know the basics
Heating and cooling systems move or produce heat to control indoor comfort. Common systems include furnaces, heat pumps, air conditioners, and portable room units.

#### Check before recommending
A thermostat controls temperature settings for compatible equipment. Air filters help capture particles in systems designed for them. Ventilation moves air between spaces or outdoors.

#### Put it into practice
Product names do not establish system compatibility. Start by asking what equipment the customer owns and what task they are trying to complete.

**Hardware-store example:** A customer asks for a “heater filter.” Ask whether they have a furnace, heat pump air handler, or portable unit.

**What to say or do:** Say: “Do you know the equipment model, or can you show the filter label?”

**Common mistakes:** Assuming every heating system uses the same filter.

**When to ask for help:** Escalate unfamiliar equipment, combustion systems, or mechanical problems to a qualified technician.

**Key takeaways:**
- Heating and cooling systems move or produce heat to control indoor comfort.
- A thermostat controls temperature settings for compatible equipment.
- Product names do not establish system compatibility.

### HB-103-L02: Help Customers Select Air Filters (5 minutes)

**Objective:** Read filter size and performance information

**Why this matters:** The wrong filter may not fit or suit the equipment.

#### Know the basics
Filter labels often list a nominal size and an actual size. These can differ. Compare the existing filter markings and equipment instructions.

#### Check before recommending
Filter performance may be described using MERV ratings. Higher numbers generally indicate greater particle capture under the rating method, but not every system can use every filter.

#### Put it into practice
Airflow requirements matter. Do not claim that the highest-rated filter is always best. Check manufacturer guidance when performance or compatibility is unclear.

**Hardware-store example:** A customer wants the thickest, highest-rated filter available. Ask for the system’s recommended size and filter specifications first.

**What to say or do:** Say: “Let’s match the size and check what your equipment allows.”

**Common mistakes:** Choosing solely by MERV rating or nominal dimensions.

**When to ask for help:** Escalate airflow complaints or uncertainty about approved filters to HVAC support.

**Key takeaways:**
- Filter labels often list a nominal size and an actual size.
- Filter performance may be described using MERV ratings.
- Airflow requirements matter.

### HB-103-L03: Recognize Thermostat and Control Differences (5 minutes)

**Objective:** Gather compatibility details

**Why this matters:** A thermostat that looks similar may not work with a particular system.

#### Know the basics
Thermostats vary by supported equipment type, voltage, wiring, and features. Some systems use proprietary controls. A basic thermostat is not automatically compatible with a heat pump or multistage system.

#### Check before recommending
Ask for the HVAC equipment model and the existing thermostat model. Manufacturer compatibility tools can help, but wiring questions belong to qualified support.

#### Put it into practice
Never instruct a customer to connect unknown wires or bypass a safety control. Do not assume wire color alone identifies its function.

**Hardware-store example:** A customer wants a smart thermostat for a heat pump. Ask for model details and point them to the manufacturer’s compatibility process.

**What to say or do:** Say: “We need to confirm your system and wiring requirements before recommending a thermostat.”

**Common mistakes:** Claiming compatibility based on thermostat appearance.

**When to ask for help:** Escalate unknown wiring, line-voltage controls, and system modifications.

**Key takeaways:**
- Thermostats vary by supported equipment type, voltage, wiring, and features.
- Ask for the HVAC equipment model and the existing thermostat model.
- Never instruct a customer to connect unknown wires or bypass a safety control.

### HB-103-L04: Compare Portable Comfort Products (5 minutes)

**Objective:** Ask questions about intended use

**Why this matters:** Portable heaters and fans have different limitations and hazards.

#### Know the basics
Fans move air but do not necessarily lower the room’s air temperature. Portable air conditioners require appropriate venting and electrical supply. Dehumidifiers remove moisture from air.

#### Check before recommending
Portable heaters require careful attention to manufacturer clearances, electrical ratings, and use restrictions. They should not be recommended with extension cords or power strips unless the manufacturer explicitly permits the setup.

#### Put it into practice
Ask about room size, moisture, outlet availability, and intended location. Do not promise a particular temperature change without verified product data.

**Hardware-store example:** A customer wants a space heater for a small workshop. Discuss the listed room application and safety clearances, then review the manufacturer instructions.

**What to say or do:** Ask: “Where will you use it, and what does the product label say about the space and power supply?”

**Common mistakes:** Recommending a heater based only on advertised wattage.

**When to ask for help:** Escalate unsafe wiring, fuel-burning heaters, or unusual installation needs.

**Key takeaways:**
- Fans move air but do not necessarily lower the room’s air temperature.
- Portable heaters require careful attention to manufacturer clearances, electrical ratings, and use restrictions.
- Ask about room size, moisture, outlet availability, and intended location.

### HB-103-L05: Recognize HVAC Warning Signs (5 minutes)

**Objective:** Refer hazardous symptoms promptly

**Why this matters:** Some comfort complaints indicate safety issues, not accessory needs.

#### Know the basics
Gas odors, carbon monoxide alarm activation, suspected refrigerant leaks, and electrical burning smells are not routine retail troubleshooting questions.

#### Check before recommending
Carbon monoxide is a dangerous gas that people cannot reliably detect by smell. A CO alarm requires following emergency guidance and contacting appropriate responders. Never suggest ignoring or disabling an alarm.

#### Put it into practice
Employees can identify packaged accessories and explain labels. They should not diagnose combustion equipment, handle refrigerants, or coach unsafe repairs.

**Hardware-store example:** A customer says a carbon monoxide alarm sounded near a gas furnace. Do not recommend a new filter as the solution.

**What to say or do:** Say: “Follow the alarm’s emergency instructions and contact emergency services or the appropriate utility or qualified professional.”

**Common mistakes:** Treating a CO alarm as a minor equipment nuisance.

**When to ask for help:** Escalate gas, CO, refrigerant, combustion, and serious electrical concerns immediately.

**Supporting material:** Safety decision graphic showing CO alarm and qualified-help handoff — **[Needed; not embedded]**. Placement: after lesson content. Alt text draft: Safety decision graphic showing CO alarm and qualified-help handoff. Licensed image/technical verification required.

**Key takeaways:**
- Gas odors, carbon monoxide alarm activation, suspected refrigerant leaks, and electrical burning smells are not routine retail troubleshooting questions.
- Carbon monoxide is a dangerous gas that people cannot reliably detect by smell.
- Employees can identify packaged accessories and explain labels.

**Unscored practice HB-103-P05:** A customer reports a carbon monoxide alarm. What should you recommend?
- A. Ignore it if the house feels warm
- B. Follow emergency guidance and seek appropriate help
- C. Change the thermostat setting
- D. Cover the alarm
**Answer:** B. **Explanation:** A CO alarm is a safety warning requiring appropriate emergency action.
