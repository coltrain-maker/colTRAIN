### HB-101-L01: Recognize Common Electrical Products (5 minutes)

**Objective:** Identify basic electrical merchandise

**Why this matters:** Customers often know what stopped working but not the name of the part.

#### Know the basics
Electrical aisles may include switches, receptacles, cover plates, cords, connectors, breakers, and lighting products. A receptacle is the outlet where a plug connects. A switch controls a circuit or device. These items have different purposes.

#### Check before recommending
Look for the product label and any rating printed on the package. A product that looks similar may have different electrical ratings or installation requirements. Do not assume that matching color or shape means matching function.

#### Put it into practice
Your job is to identify the category and gather information. Never tell a customer to work on energized equipment or bypass a protective device.

**Hardware-store example:** A customer asks for “the thing the lamp plugs into.” Ask whether they mean a wall receptacle, an extension cord, or a power strip before walking them to an aisle.

**What to say or do:** Ask: “What are you connecting, and can you describe the part you need?” Show labeled product categories without claiming compatibility.

**Common mistakes:** Handing over a look-alike electrical part without checking its label.

**When to ask for help:** If the customer describes damaged wiring, sparking, heat, or repeated breaker trips, stop product matching and direct them to qualified help.

**Key takeaways:**
- Electrical aisles may include switches, receptacles, cover plates, cords, connectors, breakers, and lighting products.
- Look for the product label and any rating printed on the package.
- Your job is to identify the category and gather information.

### HB-101-L02: Read Electrical Labels Without Guessing (5 minutes)

**Objective:** Locate ratings and compatibility information

**Why this matters:** Similar-looking electrical products may be designed for different conditions.

#### Know the basics
Packages may show voltage, amperage, wattage, location rating, or compatible equipment. Voltage describes electrical potential. Amperage relates to current. Wattage describes power use.

#### Check before recommending
These values are not a license to choose a replacement by appearance. A correct choice also depends on the existing circuit, equipment, installation, and applicable codes.

#### Put it into practice
Point to the label, read the exact wording, and compare it with verified requirements. If the customer cannot confirm those requirements, do not guess.

**Hardware-store example:** A customer wants a replacement breaker based only on its color. Explain that the exact panel and manufacturer compatibility must be confirmed.

**What to say or do:** Say: “Let’s find the model information and check the manufacturer’s listed compatibility.”

**Common mistakes:** Claiming that any breaker with the same amperage will fit.

**When to ask for help:** Escalate breaker selection, wiring alterations, unclear ratings, and code questions to a qualified electrician or authorized specialist.

### HB-101-L03: Understand Common Lighting Choices (5 minutes)

**Objective:** Compare bulb and fixture information

**Why this matters:** A customer may need a specific light output, fit, or use location.

#### Know the basics
Lighting labels often list lumens, watts, color temperature, and base type. Lumens describe light output. Watts describe energy use. Color temperature describes whether the light appears warmer or cooler.

#### Check before recommending
Check the fixture label for bulb type, maximum permitted wattage, and other restrictions. A bulb must physically fit and meet the fixture’s instructions. An enclosed fixture may require a bulb rated for enclosed use.

#### Put it into practice
Dimmable lamps and dimmer controls must be compatible. Outdoor or damp locations require products rated for that environment.

**Hardware-store example:** A customer says their new LED bulb flickers on a dimmer. Ask for the bulb and dimmer models instead of promising that a different wattage will fix it.

**What to say or do:** Ask: “Is this on a dimmer? Is the fixture enclosed? What base does your current bulb use?”

**Common mistakes:** Choosing a bulb using only the old bulb’s wattage.

**When to ask for help:** Refer uncertain fixture restrictions or dimmer compatibility to manufacturer documentation or qualified help.

**Key takeaways:**
- Lighting labels often list lumens, watts, color temperature, and base type.
- Check the fixture label for bulb type, maximum permitted wattage, and other restrictions.
- Dimmable lamps and dimmer controls must be compatible.

### HB-101-L04: Match Products to Location and Use (5 minutes)

**Objective:** Recognize environmental and use restrictions

**Why this matters:** An indoor product may be unsafe in a wet or exposed location.

#### Know the basics
Product packages may specify dry, damp, or wet locations. These labels are not interchangeable. Ask where the item will be installed and whether it will face rain, moisture, or other exposure.

#### Check before recommending
Extension cords and power strips have intended uses and ratings. Do not suggest running cords through walls, hiding them under rugs, or using them as permanent wiring.

#### Put it into practice
When a customer requests a solution for a bathroom, garage, porch, or outdoor project, the location matters as much as the product name.

**Hardware-store example:** A customer wants a porch light. Ask whether the fixture will be directly exposed to rain and confirm the manufacturer’s location rating.

**What to say or do:** Say: “Let’s check the package for the installation location it permits.”

**Common mistakes:** Assuming a product is outdoor-rated because it looks sturdy.

**When to ask for help:** Ask a qualified electrician about wiring, circuit design, grounding, and code compliance.

- Product packages may specify dry, damp, or wet locations.
- Extension cords and power strips have intended uses and ratings.
- When a customer requests a solution for a bathroom, garage, porch, or outdoor project, the location matters as much as the product name.

### HB-101-L05: Know When Electrical Questions Need an Expert (5 minutes)

**Objective:** Use a safe retail handoff

**Why this matters:** Electrical mistakes can cause fire, shock, or injury.

#### Know the basics
Employees can help locate packaged products and explain terms shown on labels. They should not diagnose live wiring, instruct customers to modify panels, or promise that a repair is code-compliant.

#### Check before recommending
Listen for warning signs: burning smells, warm outlets, sparking, repeated tripping, water near electrical equipment, or exposed conductors. Do not suggest trial-and-error repairs.

#### Put it into practice
Explain the limit without dismissing the customer. Summarize what you know and recommend a licensed electrician or appropriate emergency response for immediate danger.

**Hardware-store example:** A customer reports an outlet that sparked and smells burned. Do not sell a replacement outlet as a complete solution.

**What to say or do:** Say: “That could be hazardous. Please stop using it and contact a qualified electrician. If there is immediate danger, follow emergency guidance.”

**Common mistakes:** Treating a potentially dangerous symptom as a simple parts sale.

**When to ask for help:** Escalate hazards immediately according to store policy; use emergency services for an active emergency.

**Supporting material:** Decision diagram: product identification vs hazardous electrical symptom — **[Needed; not embedded]**. Placement: after lesson content. Alt text draft: Decision diagram: product identification vs hazardous electrical symptom. Licensed image/technical verification required.

**Key takeaways:**
- Employees can help locate packaged products and explain terms shown on labels.
- Listen for warning signs: burning smells, warm outlets, sparking, repeated tripping, water near electrical equipment, or exposed conductors.
- Explain the limit without dismissing the customer.

**Unscored practice HB-101-P05:** A customer reports a hot outlet with a burning smell. What is the best response?
- A. Suggest a larger power strip
- B. Tell them to keep testing it
- C. Advise stopping use and seeking qualified help
- D. Offer a bulb instead
**Answer:** C. **Explanation:** Heat and burning smells are warning signs; a retail employee should not diagnose or prescribe a repair.
