## 4. Full lessons

### LG-102-L01: Recognize Common Outdoor Power Equipment
**Time:** 5 minutes  
**Objective:** Match broad equipment categories to customer jobs.

**Why this matters**
A customer may know the task but not the tool name.

**Equipment families**
Mowers cut grass. String trimmers cut grass near obstacles. Edgers define edges. Blowers move loose debris. Hedge trimmers shape hedges. Chainsaws cut wood within their intended use.

**Ask about the job**
Find out the material, area size, terrain, and frequency of use. A tool built for a small residential task may not suit a large property or heavy use.

**Respect operating limits**
Every machine has intended uses and restrictions. Do not suggest removing guards, defeating safety devices, or using attachments the manufacturer has not approved.

**Hardware-store example**
A customer says they need something to clear grass around fence posts. Ask whether they mean grass trimming or edging before showing options.

**What to say or do**
Ask: “What are you cutting, and where will you use the machine?”

**Common mistakes**
Recommending a chainsaw for trimming grass; confusing a string trimmer with a hedge trimmer.

**When to ask for help**
Refer unclear or hazardous cutting tasks to an experienced equipment specialist.

### LG-102-L02: Compare Battery, Corded, and Engine Power
**Time:** 5 minutes  
**Objective:** Explain basic power-source tradeoffs without making promises.

**Why this matters**
Power source affects convenience, runtime, noise, upkeep, and where equipment can be used.

**Battery equipment**
Battery tools need a compatible battery and charger. Runtime depends on the model, battery, workload, and conditions. Batteries are not necessarily interchangeable between brands or systems.

**Corded equipment**
Corded tools require a suitable electrical supply and properly rated cords when allowed by the manufacturer. The cord limits range and introduces trip and damage hazards.

**Engine equipment**
Gasoline-powered equipment has fuel, exhaust, noise, and maintenance requirements. Never operate combustion-engine equipment indoors or in enclosed spaces due to carbon monoxide risk.

**Hardware-store example**
A customer wants a blower for a small patio and asks if any battery will fit. Explain that compatibility must be verified for the specific tool system.

**What to say or do**
Say: “Let’s check the model, compatible battery, and expected use.”

**Common mistakes**
Claiming every battery fits every tool; promising a fixed runtime for all conditions.

**When to ask for help**
Ask a specialist about battery compatibility, charging faults, fuel requirements, or electrical supply questions.

**Key takeaways**
- Battery tools need a compatible battery and charger.

### LG-102-L03: Match Equipment to the Customer’s Property
**Time:** 5 minutes  
**Objective:** Gather practical specifications before recommending a model.

**Why this matters**
The wrong size or feature set can make a job difficult or unsafe.

**Measure the job**
Ask about property size, slope, obstacles, storage space, and how often the equipment will be used. Avoid promising that one machine suits every yard.

**Compare relevant features**
For mowers, compare cutting width and manufacturer-approved terrain limitations. For blowers, look at the published performance specifications and intended application. For saws, do not treat bar length alone as a measure of safe capability.

**Confirm accessories**
Check which batteries, chargers, cutting attachments, protective equipment, and replacement parts are included or sold separately. Use the manufacturer’s compatibility list.

**Hardware-store example**
A customer wants a mower for a sloped yard. Ask about the slope and compare only models whose manuals support the intended conditions.

**What to say or do**
Ask: “How large is the area? Is it flat or sloped? Are there gates or tight spaces?”

**Common mistakes**
Choosing by horsepower or price alone; ignoring slope limitations and included accessories.

**When to ask for help**
Escalate steep terrain, large trees, overhead hazards, and unclear equipment limits.

### LG-102-L04: Explain Maintenance and Storage Safely
**Time:** 5 minutes  
**Objective:** Direct customers to model-specific care instructions.

**Why this matters**
Correct maintenance helps equipment work reliably and prevents avoidable hazards.

**Start with the manual**
Maintenance varies by engine, battery system, and tool model. Refer to the operator manual for cleaning, inspection, service intervals, and approved parts.

**Storage matters**
Customers should follow manufacturer guidance for battery storage, fuel handling, and seasonal storage. Never invent a fuel mix ratio or recommend storing fuel near heat or ignition sources.

**Know your role**
Retail employees may help locate approved parts and manuals. Repairs, blade servicing, and other hazardous work should be handled by trained authorized personnel as appropriate.

**Hardware-store example**
A customer asks what fuel mixture to use in a trimmer. Ask for the exact model and check the manufacturer manual instead of giving a universal ratio.

**What to say or do**
Say: “Fuel requirements vary. Let’s verify the exact model instructions before you add anything.”

**Common mistakes**
Assuming all two-stroke engines use the same fuel ratio; handling sharp blades without training.

**When to ask for help**
Refer repairs, battery damage, leaking fuel, and complex maintenance to trained service personnel.

### LG-102-L05: Recognize Equipment Safety and Service Limits
**Time:** 5 minutes  
**Objective:** Explain safety boundaries and escalate hazardous situations.

**Why this matters**
Outdoor power tools can cause serious injuries when used incorrectly.

**Personal protection**
Manufacturer instructions may require eye, hearing, hand, foot, or other protection depending on the machine. Follow the specific manual and store policy.

**Keep guards in place**
Do not recommend bypassing interlocks, removing guards, using damaged batteries, or operating a tool with a missing safety component.

**Escalate risky work**
Tree work near power lines, damaged electrical equipment, unstable slopes, fuel leaks, and active mechanical faults are not routine retail consultations. Direct customers to qualified professionals and appropriate emergency help when needed.

**Hardware-store example**
A customer asks how to disable a mower safety switch that keeps stopping the engine. Decline to give bypass instructions and refer them to authorized service.

**What to say or do**
Say: “That safety device must stay functional. Let’s find the approved service option.”

**Common mistakes**
Giving instructions to defeat a guard or switch; offering unverified repair steps.

**When to ask for help**
Escalate immediately when there is leaking fuel, a swollen battery, an electrical hazard, or unsafe cutting conditions.

**Key takeaways**

- Do not recommend bypassing interlocks, removing guards, using damaged batteries, or operating a tool with a missing safety component.
- Tree work near power lines, damaged electrical equipment, unstable slopes, fuel leaks, and active mechanical faults are not routine retail consultations.
