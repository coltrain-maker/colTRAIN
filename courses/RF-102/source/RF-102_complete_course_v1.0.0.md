## 4. Individual lessons

### Lesson 1 — Speaking the Language of the Store (RF-102-L01)

**Estimated time:** 4 minutes  
**Learning objective:** Explain common store and inventory terms in everyday language.

#### Why this matters
New employees hear many new words on their first shift. Clear terms help the team avoid mistakes. They also help customers feel understood.

#### Lesson content

**Everyday retail terms**

A **department** is a group of related products. Paint and plumbing are examples. An **aisle** is a walkway between shelves. A **bay** is one section of shelving. A **bin** is a container or small storage location. A **display** presents products for customers to see.

**How products move**

**Receiving** means checking products delivered to the store. **Stocking** means placing products in their assigned locations. **Replenishment** means adding more products to a shelf or display. A **return** is merchandise brought back by a customer under the store’s return policy.

**Store paperwork and labels**

A **price tag** shows a selling price. A **shelf label** identifies a product and may show its price. A **receipt** records a sale. A **purchase order** is a business record used to request products from a supplier. The exact forms vary by store.

#### Hardware-store example
A coworker says, “Please replenish the fastener bins in bay three.” The employee finds the correct shelving section and adds the right products to the assigned bins.

#### What to say or do

- Ask a coworker to show you the store’s aisle, bay, and bin labels.
- Repeat an unfamiliar instruction in your own words.
- Check product labels before stocking.

#### Common mistakes

- Treating an aisle number and a bay number as the same thing. Ask how your store labels each area.
- Assuming every store uses the same terms. Learn the terms used by your team.

#### When to ask for help
Ask a manager or experienced coworker when a location code is unclear. Do not move merchandise based on a guess.

#### Supporting materials
**Actual media:** None. **Suggested media:** None required. Text-only launch is supported.

#### Key takeaways

- Ask a coworker to show you the store’s aisle, bay, and bin labels.
- Repeat an unfamiliar instruction in your own words.
- Ask a manager or experienced coworker when a location code is unclear.

- **C.** Change all product prices.
- **D.** Process a customer return.

**Answer:** B

### Lesson 2 — Inventory Words You Will Hear (RF-102-L02)

**Estimated time:** 4 minutes  
**Learning objective:** Explain common store and inventory terms in everyday language.

#### Why this matters
Inventory words appear in conversations, labels, and store systems. You need to understand them before you can answer stock questions.

#### Lesson content

**Item identifiers**

A **SKU** is a stock keeping unit. It is an identifier a business uses to track an item. A **UPC** is a Universal Product Code. It is commonly shown as a barcode number on retail packaging. A store SKU and a UPC may be different. Some products have more than one barcode or packaging option.

**Stock and availability**

**On-hand quantity** is the number recorded in the inventory system. **Available quantity** may account for reserved or committed stock. Stores calculate these values in different ways. **Out of stock** means the item is not currently available for sale at that location. **Backorder** means an item has been ordered but is waiting for supply. Not every store uses backorders.

**Ordering terms**

A **special order** is a product ordered for a specific customer request. A **lead time** is the expected time between ordering and receiving an item. An **inventory count** checks the actual number of items. An **inventory adjustment** corrects a recorded quantity after the store verifies a difference.

#### Hardware-store example
The system shows two boxes of screws on hand. The shelf is empty. An employee checks approved storage locations and asks receiving for help. They do not promise the customer that two boxes are ready to buy.

#### What to say or do

- Use “the system shows” when describing an unverified quantity.
- Check approved locations before confirming availability.
- Explain that special-order timing is an estimate until the store confirms it.

#### Common mistakes

- Confusing SKU with UPC. Check the field label in the system.
- Treating on-hand quantity as proof that an item is on the shelf. Verify the item.
- Promising an arrival date from an unconfirmed lead time.

#### When to ask for help
Ask an inventory lead or manager about quantity differences, reserved stock, and order status. Follow the store’s process for adjustments.

#### Supporting materials
**Actual media:** None. **Suggested media:** None required. Text-only launch is supported.

#### Key takeaways

- Use “the system shows” when describing an unverified quantity.
- Check approved locations before confirming availability.
- Ask an inventory lead or manager about quantity differences, reserved stock, and order status.

### Lesson 3 — Product Names, Sizes, and Units (RF-102-L03)

**Estimated time:** 4 minutes  
**Learning objective:** Recognize basic product names, sizes, and units used in hardware retail.

#### Why this matters
Hardware products often look alike. Small differences in size or material can change how a product works. Reading the label is an important skill.

#### Lesson content

**Common product families**

A **fastener** joins materials. Screws, nails, nuts, and bolts are fasteners. A **fitting** connects parts of a system. Plumbing fittings are one example. A **consumable** is used up during work. Sandpaper and drill bits may need regular replacement. A **hand tool** uses human effort. A **power tool** uses a motor or another power source.

**Measurements and labels**

**Length**, **width**, and **diameter** describe different dimensions. **Diameter** measures across a circle through its center. **Gauge** is a size designation used for some products. Its meaning depends on the product category. A **thread** is the spiral ridge on a screw or bolt. A **thread size** helps identify a matching fastener. The numbers and units on the package matter.

**Units and package counts**

Stores sell products by **each**, **pair**, **pack**, **foot**, **gallon**, or other units. A price per each is not the same as a price per pack. **Nominal size** is a common name for a material size. It may differ from the actual measured size. Lumber is one example. Check the label and product specifications.

#### Hardware-store example
A customer asks for “a half-inch bolt.” The employee asks for the old bolt or package. They check diameter, length, thread information, and grade or strength markings as needed. They do not choose a bolt based on length alone.

#### What to say or do

- Read the full product label.
- Confirm the unit of sale before quoting a price.
- Ask for the old part, package, or a clear photo when available.

#### Common mistakes

- Assuming two similar-looking fasteners are interchangeable. Check the full specifications.
- Confusing diameter with length. Identify each measurement.
- Assuming nominal and actual lumber sizes are equal. Verify the dimensions.

#### When to ask for help
Ask a knowledgeable coworker or consult manufacturer specifications when strength, compatibility, load, electrical ratings, or other safety factors matter. Refer installation decisions to a qualified professional when appropriate.

#### Supporting materials
**Actual media:** None. **Suggested media:** None required. Text-only launch is supported.

#### Key takeaways

- Read the full product label.
- Confirm the unit of sale before quoting a price.
- Ask a knowledgeable coworker or consult manufacturer specifications when strength, compatibility, load, electrical ratings, or other safety factors matter.

### Lesson 4 — Department Vocabulary (RF-102-L04)

**Estimated time:** 4 minutes  
**Learning objective:** Use common department terms to direct customers without guessing.

#### Why this matters
Customers may use everyday words instead of product names. Department vocabulary helps you ask better questions and find the right team member.

#### Lesson content

**Paint and decorating**

**Primer** prepares a surface for a coating. **Sheen** describes how shiny a paint finish looks. **Roller cover** is the sleeve that holds paint on a roller frame. **Caulk** fills certain gaps or joints. Products have different uses. Read the label.

**Plumbing and electrical**

A **shutoff valve** controls water flow. A **supply line** carries water to a fixture. A **coupling** joins two compatible pipe or tubing sections. An **outlet** is a place to connect electrical equipment. A **breaker** is a protective device in an electrical circuit. Employees should not diagnose electrical hazards or direct unqualified work.

**Lawn, garden, and tools**

**Mulch** covers soil. **Potting mix** is a growing medium for containers. **Trimmer line** is cutting line used by certain string trimmers. A **driver bit** turns compatible screws. A **drill bit** makes holes in suitable material. Check tool and accessory compatibility before recommending a product.

#### Hardware-store example
A customer asks for “the string for my weed eater.” The employee recognizes that the customer may mean trimmer line. They ask for the model or the old package before checking compatible sizes.

#### What to say or do

- Translate the customer’s everyday words into possible product terms.
- Ask for a model number when fit or compatibility matters.
- Direct the customer to the right department or coworker.

#### Common mistakes

- Assuming all products with similar names have the same purpose. Read labels.
- Treating a familiar brand name as an exact product description. Ask for details.

#### When to ask for help
Ask a department specialist or consult official product instructions for chemical use, electrical work, plumbing repairs, and equipment compatibility. Do not provide unverified safety advice.

#### Supporting materials

#### Key takeaways

- Translate the customer’s everyday words into possible product terms.
- Ask for a model number when fit or compatibility matters.

### Lesson 5 — Clear Communication and Smart Questions (RF-102-L05)

**Estimated time:** 4 minutes  
**Learning objective:** Use clear terms when communicating about stock, locations, and customer needs.

#### Why this matters
Knowing a term is only part of the job. Clear communication helps coworkers act on the right information. It also helps customers avoid buying the wrong item.

#### Lesson content

**Be specific**

Instead of “We need more of those,” say “The one-inch paint brushes are low on the shelf in aisle four.” Instead of “The order is here,” say “The system shows the special order as received. I will verify it before contacting the customer.”

**Use a simple question pattern**

Ask **what** the customer needs to do. Ask **where** the product will be used. Ask **which** size, model, or material is involved. Ask **whether** they have the old part, package, or instructions. One clear question is better than several guesses.

**Know the limits of a term**

Words like **universal**, **standard**, and **heavy-duty** may appear on packages. They do not prove that a product fits every project. Read the manufacturer’s specifications. If a term is unclear, say what you know and what you still need to check.

#### Hardware-store example
A customer says, “I need a universal blade for this saw.” The employee asks for the saw model and the type of work. They check the blade specifications and seek help before recommending a product.

#### What to say or do

- Use exact product names and location details when possible.
- Tell customers when a detail still needs verification.
- Pass clear notes to the next employee.

#### Common mistakes

- Using vague words like “that one” without a product description. Name the item.
- Assuming “universal” means guaranteed compatibility. Check the specifications.
- Pretending to understand an unfamiliar term. Ask for clarification.

#### When to ask for help
Ask a manager or specialist when a product label, model number, or safety claim is unclear. Refer regulated work to a qualified professional as appropriate.

#### Supporting materials
**Actual media:** None. **Suggested media:** None required. Text-only launch is supported.

#### Key takeaways

- Use exact product names and location details when possible.
- Tell customers when a detail still needs verification.
- Ask a manager or specialist when a product label, model number, or safety claim is unclear.
